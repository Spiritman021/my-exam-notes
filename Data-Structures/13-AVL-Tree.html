Chapter 13

AVL Tree

Chapter 12 ended with a problem: insert sorted keys into a BST and it degrades into a linked list with O(n) search. An AVL tree (named after Adelson-Velsky and Landis, 1962) checks the balance after every insert and delete and repairs it with rotations, so the height always stays O(log n). The lecture notes that it is more complex than the trees so far, but many questions are asked on it.

13.1Self-Balancing BST and Balance Factor (−1, 0, +1)

🎓 From the lecture An AVL tree is an evolved version of the BST. All the BST rules apply, plus one more property: it is self-balancing, i.e. it balances itself. That property adds a term, the balance factor:
Balance factor (n) = height(left subtree of n) − height(right subtree of n) Allowed values: −1, 0, +1
  • A root with one left and one right child: 1 − 1 = 0 ✔.
  • Add a node under the right child: 1 − 2 = −1 ✔.
  • Left height 3, right height 2: 3 − 2 = +1 ✔.
Those are the three allowed conditions. Anything else (±2) means the balance is broken and must be fixed.
⚠️ Exam trap
  • Use left − right, as the lecture does: a positive BF means left-heavy.
  • An empty subtree has height −1 when a leaf has height 0. The lecture simply counts the levels on each side, which gives the same differences.
  • Every node, not just the root, must have BF in {−1, 0, +1}.

13.2Single Rotations: LL (Right Rotate) and RR (Left Rotate)

🎓 From the lecture
  • Left rotate (RR problem): A → B was fine: BF(A) = 0 − 1 = −1. Then C was inserted to the right of B. The impact falls on A, whose BF becomes −2 (magnitude 2). The cause: a node was inserted in the right of the right. Fix: rotate left. B moves up; A becomes B's left child and C its right child, and every BF is 0.
  • Right rotate (LL problem): C was inserted in the left of the left of A, so A is unbalanced. "If there's so much trouble on the left, rotate to the right." B moves up, with C on its left and A on its right.
In short: an RR problem is neutralised by a left rotation, and an LL problem by a right rotation.

13.3Double Rotations: LR and RL

🎓 From the lecture These two are slightly more complicated.
  • LR problem: B is A's left child, and C was added to B's right, so A is unbalanced. The node went into A's left subtree, at that subtree's right, so the problem is "LR". Fix with an LR rotation:
    • on the R part (the B–C link), apply a left rotation;
    • then on the L part (at A), apply a right rotation.
    Result: C on top, B on its left, A on its right.
  • RL problem: B is A's right child, and C was added to B's left, so A's BF reaches 2 in magnitude. Fix with an RL rotation:
    • first a right rotation at B;
    • then a left rotation at A.
    Result: C on top, A on its left, B on its right.
Memory rule: wherever you see an R, apply a left rotation; wherever you see an L, apply a right rotation, starting from the lower one.
CaseNew node went into…BF of unbalanced node / of its childFix
LLleft subtree of the left child+2 / +1single right rotation
RRright subtree of the right child−2 / −1single left rotation
LRright subtree of the left child+2 / −1left at the child, then right at the node
RLleft subtree of the right child−2 / +1right at the child, then left at the node
🎓 From the lecture After every rotation, check that the BST property still holds (left < parent < right). If it does, you have very probably rotated correctly; if not, you made a mistake while rotating.

13.5Deletion in an AVL Tree (13, 12, 16, 19)

🎓 From the lecture Delete as in a BST, then check every balance factor, and rotate where one breaks. Starting tree: 19(16(12(9, 13), 17(—, 18)), 24(22, 40(30))).
  1. Delete 13 (leaf): check all BFs; all are 0 or ±1, so no problem.
  2. Delete 12: 9 takes its place. Still balanced.
  3. Delete 16 (two children): "fasten your seat belts, the weather is about to turn". Replace it with 9, the key just before 16. Now node 9 has only a right chain 17 → 18, all in one line: BF −2, an RR problem, so rotate left. That gives 17(9, 18).
  4. Delete 19 (the root, two children): replace with 18. Final tree: 18, 17, 9, 24, 22, 40, 30, which is correct.
The balance factor itself tells you the nature of the problem (e.g. −2 in a straight right line = RR).
⚠️ Exam trap
  • When replacing 16 and 19, the lecture calls 9 and 18 the "inorder successor". They are actually the inorder predecessors, the largest keys in the left subtrees. 9 < 16 and 18 < 19. The successors would be 17 and 22.
  • With the successor rule, the intermediate trees differ, but both choices give a valid AVL tree. Follow the rule the question states.
  • Unlike insertion, deletion may need rotations at several levels, up to O(log n) of them on the way back to the root.

13.6Height Bounds, Minimum Nodes, Complexity & Solved QuestionsADDED

➕ Minimum nodes in an AVL tree of height h The sparsest AVL tree of height h has one subtree of height h−1 and the other of height h−2:
N(h) = N(h−1) + N(h−2) + 1, N(0) = 1, N(1) = 2 h : 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 N : 1 2 4 7 12 20 33
So the height grows like ~1.44·log₂ n: an AVL tree is never more than about 44 % taller than a perfect tree. The maximum number of nodes is the same as for any binary tree, 2^(h+1) − 1.
OperationBST (worst)AVL (worst)
SearchO(n)O(log n)
InsertO(n)O(log n), at most 1 rotation (single or double)
DeleteO(n)O(log n), up to O(log n) rotations
Single rotation—O(1): only a few pointers change
➕ AVL vs red-black AVL trees are more strictly balanced, so lookups are faster. Red-black trees allow more imbalance (height ≤ 2 log₂(n+1)) but need fewer rotations, so inserts and deletes are cheaper. Language libraries (C++ map, Java TreeMap) use red-black trees; read-heavy indexes often prefer AVL.
🎯 IBPS exam alert
  • "An AVL tree is a ____ BST" → height-balanced / self-balancing.
  • Allowed balance factors: −1, 0, +1; BF = h(left) − h(right).
  • Insertion in the left subtree of the left child → LL → single right rotation.
  • Worst-case search in an AVL tree → O(log n).
  • Minimum nodes in an AVL tree of height 3 → 7 (4 → 12).
Q1Insert 10, 20, 30 into an empty AVL tree. What happens? → 30 makes 10's BF −2 (RR), so left rotate. Root 20, children 10 and 30.
Q2Insert 30, 10, 20. → 20 goes to the right of 10, which is the left of 30: LR. Left rotate at 10, then right rotate at 30. Root 20.
Q3Minimum number of nodes in an AVL tree of height 5? → N(5) = N(4) + N(3) + 1 = 12 + 7 + 1 = 20.
Q4Maximum height of an AVL tree with 12 nodes? → N(4) = 12 ≤ 12 < N(5) = 20, so the maximum height is 4. A plain BST could reach 11.
Q5A node has BF +2 and its left child has BF −1. Which rotation? → LR: left rotation at the child, then right rotation at the node.

⚡ Rapid Revision

  • AVL = self-balancing BST. BF = h(left) − h(right) ∈ {−1, 0, +1} at every node.
  • Name the case by the path from the unbalanced node towards the new node: LL → right, RR → left, LR → left at child, then right, RL → right at child, then left.
  • After any rotation the middle key goes on top; recheck the BST order.
  • Lecture example 21, 26, 30, 9, 4, 14, 28, 18, 15, 10, 2, 3, 7: RR, LL, LR, RL, RL, RL, LL, LR, LR. Final root 14, height 3.
  • Deletion: BST delete, then rebalance (it may cascade). Lecture: deleting 16 causes RR at 9 → 17(9, 18). Final preorder 18 17 9 24 22 40 30. (9 and 18 are predecessors, though the lecture says "successor".)
  • Min nodes N(h) = N(h−1) + N(h−2) + 1: 1, 2, 4, 7, 12, 20, 33. Height ≈ 1.44 log₂ n. All operations are O(log n).
