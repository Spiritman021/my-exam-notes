Chapter 14

Heap

A heap is the tree behind the priority queue from chapter 9. It doesn't keep everything sorted like a BST; it only guarantees that the largest (or smallest) element is at the root. That weaker promise is cheap to maintain: O(log n) per insert or delete, in a plain array with no pointers.

14.1Min Heap and Max Heap (Complete Binary Tree + Heap Property)

🎓 From the lecture A heap is first a complete binary tree, filled level by level from left to right (chapter 11). The "popular example" of a tree that is not complete is one where a right child was filled while its left sibling slot was empty: "if you had to fill, why fill that one? You should have put the left one."
On top of that comes one property that a BST doesn't have, the parent–child relationship:
  • Min heap: the parent is always less than its children. Example: 2 < 3, 4; 3 < 5, 6; 4 < 9, 8.
  • Max heap: the parent is always greater than its children. Example: 50 > 20, 30; 20 > 10, 5; 30 > 15, 18.
The property must hold throughout the tree, not just at one node or subtree.
⚠️ Exam trap A heap orders only parent vs child; there is no left–right order. In the max heap above, 10 is on the left and 30 on the right at different levels, and that's fine. So an inorder walk of a heap is not sorted, and searching for an arbitrary key is O(n).

14.2Heapify: Converting a Binary Tree into a Max Heap

🎓 From the lecture Heapify is a famous process: given any binary tree, convert it into a max heap or min heap, as the question asks. The great quality of a heap is that the root gives the maximum (max heap) or the minimum (min heap) of all the data.
Method: start from the lowest subtrees and work up to the root, checking the heap property at every level.
  1. Subtree 2 → 5: in a max heap the parent must be bigger, so swap, giving 5 → 2.
  2. Subtree 9 → (1, 4): 9 is bigger than both. Fine.
  3. Root 3 with children 9 and 5: "were we making a min heap or a max heap?" Swap 9 and 3.
  4. 3 went down and now sits above 4, a child bigger than its parent. Swap again: 4 goes up, 3 goes down.
Now every level satisfies the max-heap property.
➕ Added for exam This is sift-down (also called percolate down). Whenever a node is swapped down, keep pushing it down until it is larger than both its children or becomes a leaf. Doing this for every internal node from the last one up to the root is the standard build-heap, and it costs only O(n).

14.3Max Heap Example 2 (Choose the Larger Child)

🎓 From the lecture Input (level order): 10, 20, 25, 6, 12, 15, 4, 16.
  1. Lowest: 6 → 16. Swap, so 16 goes up.
  2. Subtree 20 → (16, 12): fine. Subtree 25 → (15, 4): fine.
  3. Root 10 is less than both 20 and 25. A new case: which one goes up? Take the maximum of the two, because whatever goes up must be greater than both children. Swap 10 ↔ 25.
  4. 10 now sits above 15. A child can't be bigger than its parent, so swap 10 ↔ 15.
⚠️ Exam trap When the parent breaks the rule with both children, swap with the larger child (max heap) or the smaller child (min heap). Swapping 10 with 20 here would leave 20 above 25, so the root would still be wrong.

14.4Min Heap from the Same Input (Choose the Smaller Child)

🎓 From the lecture Same input, now building a min heap, again from the lowest point upwards:
  1. 6 → 16: the parent is smaller. Fine for a min heap.
  2. 20 → (6, 12): 20 must not be the bigger parent, so swap with 6. 20 is still bigger than its new child 16, so swap again: 6 → 16 → 20.
  3. 25 → (15, 4): 4 goes up, 25 goes down.
  4. Root 10 with children 6 and 4: two options again. This time choose the minimum (4), because the root must be the smallest. Swap, so 4 is the root and 10 goes to its right.
"That's why a min heap gives us the minimum element of all the data."

14.5Array Layout, Insert, Delete, Heap Sort & Solved QuestionsADDED

➕ Stored in an array A heap is complete, so it fits an array with no gaps. Using 0-based indexing:
children of i : 2i + 1, 2i + 2 parent of i : ⌊(i − 1) / 2⌋ (1-based: 2i, 2i + 1 and ⌊i / 2⌋ — chapter 11) leaves : indices ⌊n/2⌋ … n − 1 (the last ⌈n/2⌉ elements)
In a max heap the minimum is always one of the leaves.
➕ Insert (sift-up), O(log n) Put the new key in the next free slot (the end of the array, keeping the tree complete). While it is larger than its parent (max heap), swap it upward.
Insert 10, 20, 15, 30, 40 into an empty max heap:
10 → [10] 20 ↑ swap → [20, 10] 15 → [20, 10, 15] 30 ↑↑ → [30, 20, 15, 10] 40 ↑↑ → [40, 30, 15, 10, 20]
➕ Delete root (sift-down), O(log n) You only ever delete the root (the max or min).
  1. Copy the last element into the root, and shrink the array.
  2. Sift that element down, swapping it with its larger child (max heap).
Delete the max from [40, 30, 15, 10, 20]: 20 moves to the root and sinks below 30, giving [30, 20, 15, 10].
➕ Heap sort
  1. Build a max heap (O(n)).
  2. Repeat n − 1 times: swap the root with the last element, shrink the heap by one, and sift down (O(log n)).
Example: [12, 11, 13, 5, 6, 7] → heap [13, 11, 12, 5, 6, 7] → sorted [5, 6, 7, 11, 12, 13].
  • Time is O(n log n) in best, average and worst case.
  • It sorts in place (O(1) extra space).
  • It is not stable.

⚠️ Exam trap Bottom-up heapify and inserting one by one can give different (both valid) heaps from the same input. For 35, 33, 42, 10, 14, 19, 27, 44, 26, 31:
  • one-by-one inserts → [44, 42, 35, 33, 31, 19, 27, 10, 26, 14];
  • bottom-up build-heap → [44, 35, 42, 33, 31, 19, 27, 10, 26, 14].
Read the question: "inserted one by one" or "build/heapify the array".
🎯 IBPS exam alert
  • A heap is a complete binary tree with the heap-order property.
  • Max heap → maximum at the root; min heap → minimum at the root.
  • Best data structure for a priority queue → heap.
  • Heap sort: O(n log n) always, in place, not stable.
  • Building a heap from an array: O(n).
  • Uses: priority queues, CPU scheduling, Dijkstra's and Prim's algorithms, k largest/smallest elements, median maintenance, heap sort.
Q1Is [50, 30, 40, 10, 35, 20] a max heap? → Index 1 (30) has children 10 and 35, and 35 > 30, so No.
Q2Max heap [40, 30, 15, 10, 20]: insert 35. → 35 goes to index 5, whose parent is index 2 (15). Swap. Its new parent 40 is bigger, so stop: [40, 30, 35, 10, 20, 15].
Q3In a max heap of 15 distinct elements, where can the smallest element be? → Among the leaves, indices 7–14 (0-based): 8 positions.
Q4Height of a heap with 100 elements? → ⌊log₂ 100⌋ = 6.
Q5Time to find the minimum element in a max heap? → It must be a leaf, but any of about n/2 leaves could hold it: O(n).

⚡ Rapid Revision

  • Heap = complete binary tree (filled left → right) + heap order throughout. Max heap: parent ≥ children (root = max). Min heap: parent ≤ children (root = min).
  • Lecture examples: min heap 2(3(5, 6), 4(9, 8)); max heap 50(20(10, 5), 30(15, 18)).
  • Heapify bottom-up: fix the lowest subtrees first, then go up. A swapped-down node keeps sinking (3 ↔ 9, then 3 ↔ 4).
  • If the parent is out of order with both children, swap with the larger (max heap) or smaller (min heap). The lecture's 10, 20, 25, 6, 12, 15, 4, 16 → max root 25, min root 4.
  • Array: children at 2i + 1 and 2i + 2, parent at ⌊(i−1)/2⌋ (0-based). Insert = append and sift up. Delete root = move the last element to the root and sift down. Both O(log n).
  • Build heap O(n); heap sort O(n log n), in place, not stable. Heap = priority queue.
