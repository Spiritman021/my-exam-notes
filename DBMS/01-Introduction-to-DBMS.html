Chapter 1

Introduction to DBMS

Every bank, school, shop and app keeps records: who the customers are, what they bought, how much money sits in which account. A database management system is the software that stores those records and lets many people read and change them quickly, safely and without contradicting each other. This chapter sets up the basic vocabulary, shows why plain files on a disk were not good enough, and separates the two jobs databases do: running today's transactions (OLTP) and analysing years of history (OLAP).

1.1Data, Information, Database and DBMS (Shopping-Mart Analogy)

🎓 From the lecture
  • Data: almost everything is data. Facts, figures (aankde), statistics and records are all data, and today data is extremely valuable.
  • Information: meaningful, processed, useful and relevant data. Information is also data, but not all data is information. There is a "thin line of difference" that students often blur.

    Example: the teacher's life story

    You are studying DBMS. In the middle of the lecture the teacher starts on the sad story of his life: what he likes to eat, which people he likes and dislikes. You would ask, "Sir, what is going on? Is this useful or relevant here?" What he is saying is data, but in this context it is not information.
  • Database (DB): a collection of related data. The data can relate to one person, a group, a company, an institute or a school. Company X and Company Y each have their own employees, rules and functions, so each has its own related data, and therefore its own separate database.
  • DBMS: a software that lets you store, access, retrieve, update, create, maintain, delete and secure data.

Example: the shopping mart

A mart (vegetables, cold drinks, groceries, fashion, all under one roof) does to items exactly what a DBMS does to data:
  • Store: everything is kept on shelves.
  • Access / retrieve: chocolates, candies and chips are placed on the lowest shelves so small children can reach them and slip them into the cart before their parents notice. Nothing is locked or password-protected; you pick it up and go.
  • Update / modify: items that are not selling are replaced with another brand or item.
  • Create: some marts make products under their own brand and place them near the billing counter, so every customer sees them.
  • Maintain: milk, butter and cold drinks go in the refrigerator; other items stay on shelves.
  • Delete: items past their expiry date are removed, because keeping them plays with the customer's life.
  • Secure: all of this happens under CCTV surveillance.
DBMS: a collection of interrelated data together with a set of programs to access that data, providing a convenient and efficient way to store and retrieve database information (Korth). Examples: MySQL, Oracle, PostgreSQL, Microsoft SQL Server, IBM DB2, SQLite, MS Access. MongoDB and Cassandra are NoSQL DBMSs.
➕ Added for exam
  • Database system = database + DBMS software (+ application programs).
  • Metadata is "data about data": table names, column types, constraints. The DBMS keeps it in the data dictionary (system catalog).
  • RDBMS (relational DBMS) stores data as tables (relations). The relational model was proposed by E. F. Codd at IBM in 1970. Codd later gave 12 rules (numbered 0–12, so 13 in all) that a DBMS must follow to be called relational.
  • Main database users: DBA (database administrator), database designers, application programmers, and end users (naive, casual, sophisticated).
⚠️ Exam trap "All information is data, but all data is not information." Options that reverse this are wrong. Also, a database is the collection of data; the DBMS is the software. "Oracle is a database" is loose talk; strictly, Oracle is a DBMS.

1.2Problems with the File System: Redundancy, Inconsistency, Difficult Access, Isolation (Boys' Hostel Room)

🎓 From the lecture Before DBMS, data lived in the conventional file system. Why did we need something new? Because of these disadvantages:
  1. Data redundancy: the most basic and most common problem. You keep copies of the same files in different drives and locations. Storage is wasted, and when space runs out you can't decide what to delete, so you buy a new hard drive. Cost goes up, and with so much data the cost of access goes up too.
  2. Data inconsistency: you save the same file in Drive A and Drive B, then fix a spelling mistake in Drive B's copy. That change does not reflect in Drive A unless you do it by hand. The two copies were consistent; now they are inconsistent, purely because of how a file system works.
  3. Difficult access: you are handed the school's records for the last 10 years and asked for the full data of every student who was selected for the cricket team. Doing that by hand is very hard.
  4. Data isolation: the data is scattered all over the system, like a boys' hostel room: jeans here, socks there, towel somewhere, bag, books and laptop all over the place. Pulling out just the data you are interested in becomes a difficult task.
➕ Added for exam Textbook lists (Korth) add three more file-system problems:
  • Integrity problems: rules such as "balance must never go below ₹0" are buried inside program code. Adding or changing a rule means editing many programs.
  • Data dependence: each program depends on the exact file format. Change the file layout and every program that reads it must be rewritten. A DBMS gives data independence (chapter 2).
  • Limited data sharing: each department keeps its own files in its own format.
Advantages of DBMS are the opposites: controlled redundancy, consistency, easy querying (SQL), data sharing, integrity constraints, security via views and permissions, atomic transactions, concurrency control, and backup and recovery. Disadvantages of DBMS: high cost of software, hardware and trained staff, complexity, size, and higher impact of a failure (everything is in one place).
⚠️ Exam trap A DBMS gives controlled (minimised) redundancy, not zero redundancy. Some duplication is deliberate, such as foreign key values repeated across tables and backup copies. If an option says "DBMS eliminates redundancy completely", it is wrong.

1.3Security, Atomicity and Concurrency Issues in File Systems (Password-Protected PDF)

🎓 From the lecture
  1. Security issues: you put a password on a PDF. That authenticates the file as a whole: whoever has the password can open it. But you cannot secure the data inside it user by user: page 1 visible only to User 1, page 2 only to User 2, neither seeing the other's page. In a DBMS this is easy through views; users get different views of the same data without even knowing it.
  2. Atomicity: a task must be done completely or not at all (all or none). If a transaction fails half-way, it should be rolled back and the data restored to the original values. This is really hard in a file system. It is covered in detail with the ACID properties (chapter 16).
  3. Concurrency: "the world runs on concurrency". Many users access your server at the same time and expect a fast, efficient experience. A file system is slow at this, and redundancy makes it worse. DBMS solves it with concurrency control protocols (chapter 19).
  4. Simplicity: the file system's only plus is that it is simple. "What do I do with that simplicity?" With so many disadvantages, it is not worth much. That is why we move on to DBMS.
🎯 IBPS exam alert The standard list of file-system drawbacks to remember: redundancy, inconsistency, difficulty in accessing data, data isolation, integrity problems, atomicity problems, concurrent-access anomalies, security problems. A typical question: "Which of these is NOT a disadvantage of the file processing system?", where the odd one out is something like "data independence" or "simplicity".

1.4OLAP vs OLTP (Heaters and Blankets Sell in Winter)

🎓 From the lecture Both full forms start with Online and end with Processing; the difference is the middle letter: Analytical vs Transaction.
  • OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) needs historical data. To analyse anything you need history.
  • OLTP (Online Transaction Processing) works on current, operational, real-time data.
  • In any organisation, roughly 95–97% of its data is historical (everything since the company started 10–15 years ago) and only about 5% is current, operational data.
  • So OLAP handles a large volume and needs large storage; OLTP handles a small volume and needs small storage.
  • Applications of OLAP: analysing trends (where the high and low points are and after what period they repeat) and predicting customer behaviour. In winter you observe that heaters and blankets sell more.
  • Applications of OLTP: no heavy analysis; you manage and process payments and orders as they come in.

➕ Added for exam OLAP runs on a data warehouse, which is loaded from OLTP systems through ETL (Extract, Transform, Load). OLAP data is seen as a multi-dimensional cube, with these operations:
  • Roll-up (drill-up): summarise to a higher level, e.g. city → state.
  • Drill-down: go to finer detail, e.g. year → month.
  • Slice: fix one dimension (only the year 2025).
  • Dice: pick a sub-cube on two or more dimensions.
  • Pivot (rotate): swap rows and columns of the view.
OLAP types: ROLAP (relational), MOLAP (multidimensional), HOLAP (hybrid). In a bank, the core banking system (CBS) is OLTP; the MIS and analytics reports are OLAP.
⚠️ Exam trap Do not mix up the letters: T = Transaction = today's data; A = Analytical = archive (history). OLTP databases are normalised; data warehouses for OLAP are usually denormalised for faster reads.

1.5Structured vs Unstructured Data (Skeleton vs Amoeba)

🎓 From the lecture Broadly, data falls into two types (the lecture covers this right after the three-schema architecture):
  • Structured data: follows a fixed structure, framework or skeleton for storing and placing the data. Rules apply, and the data stays within them. Like a human or an animal, which has a specific, predefined skeleton that organs and nerves are arranged around. The relational database holds structured data: tables with rows and columns. A table is a relation of rows and columns, which is why a table is also called a relation. SQL (Structured Query Language) operates on structured data.
  • Unstructured data: follows no structure, framework or skeleton. Like an amoeba, which has no fixed shape: it looks one way today and another way tomorrow. Examples: images, audio, video.

➕ Added for exam Exams often add a third category, semi-structured data (XML, JSON). An estimated 80–90% of the world's data is unstructured. In the relational model: table = relation, row = tuple (record), column = attribute (field); the number of columns is the degree and the number of rows is the cardinality.
⚠️ Exam trap Degree = number of attributes (columns); cardinality = number of tuples (rows). A table with 5 columns and 100 rows has degree 5 and cardinality 100. Note that "cardinality" also has a second meaning in ER diagrams (1:1, 1:N, M:N), covered in chapter 3.

1.6Solved Exam QuestionsADDED

Q1Processed, meaningful and relevant data is called: (a) Metadata (b) Information (c) Database (d) Record
Answer: (b) Information. Metadata is data about data.
Q2The same customer address stored in two files, updated in only one, leads to: (a) data isolation (b) data inconsistency (c) data independence (d) atomicity
Answer: (b) Data inconsistency, caused by redundancy.
Q3Which is NOT a disadvantage of the file processing system? (a) Data redundancy (b) Data isolation (c) Data independence (d) Concurrent-access anomalies
Answer: (c) Data independence. It is an advantage of DBMS; files suffer from data dependence.
Q4A bank's ATM withdrawals are handled by which type of system?
Answer: OLTP (current, real-time transactions). Analysing five years of withdrawal patterns would be OLAP.
Q5Which OLAP operation moves from yearly sales to monthly sales?
Answer: Drill-down. The reverse (month → year) is roll-up.
Q6A JSON file is an example of: (a) structured (b) semi-structured (c) unstructured data
Answer: (b) Semi-structured.
Q7A relation has 6 attributes and 250 tuples. What are its degree and cardinality?
Answer: Degree 6, cardinality 250.
Q8Who proposed the relational model of data?
Answer: E. F. Codd (IBM, 1970).

⚡ Rapid Revision

  • Data = facts, figures, statistics, records. Information = meaningful, processed, useful, relevant data (the teacher's life story is data, not information).
  • Database = collection of related data (Company X and Y each have their own). DBMS = software to store, access, retrieve, update, create, maintain, delete and secure data (shopping-mart analogy).
  • File system drawbacks: redundancy (extra hard drive) → inconsistency (Drive A vs B) · difficult access (10 years of cricket-team records) · isolation (boys' hostel room) · security (PDF password is all-or-nothing; DBMS uses views) · atomicity (all or none, rollback) · concurrency (many users at once). Only plus: simplicity. ➕ Also integrity problems and data dependence.
  • DBMS gives controlled redundancy, not zero.
  • OLAP = analytical, historical (~95%), large volume and storage, trends and behaviour prediction (heaters in winter), data warehouse, denormalised. OLTP = transactional, current (~5%), small volume, payments and orders, normalised.
  • ➕ OLAP operations: roll-up, drill-down, slice, dice, pivot. ETL loads the warehouse.
  • Structured = fixed skeleton, tables (table = relation), SQL. Unstructured = no shape like an amoeba: images, audio, video. ➕ Semi-structured = XML, JSON.
  • ➕ Degree = columns; cardinality = rows. Relational model: E. F. Codd, 1970.
