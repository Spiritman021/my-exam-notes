Chapter 4

Requirement Engineering and Gathering

Most failed projects fail not because the code was bad but because the team built the wrong thing. Requirement engineering is the work of finding out exactly what to build before deciding how. The lecture's one-line summary: "Communication is the key." This chapter covers the challenges, the questions, the steps and the techniques. Chapter 5 covers the analysis tools and chapter 6 the SRS document they lead to.

4.1Goal of Requirement Analysis and Its Challenges

🎓 From the lecture Goal: an exact understanding of "what to build". The output is the SRS (Software Requirement Specification).

Challenges

  • Ambiguity: one statement, two meanings.
  • Inconsistency: requirements that contradict each other.
  • Incompleteness: requirements that are missing.
  • Conflicting stakeholders: stakeholder X wants one thing, stakeholder Y the opposite.
  • Requirement changes during the project.
  • Tight time and budget constraints.
  • Understanding gap: clients come from different domains (finance, sports, academics) with their own terminology, which the development team may not understand, and vice versa.

4.2Basic Questions to Ask the Client (Shark Tank)

🎓 From the lecture
  1. What is the problem?
  2. Why is it important to solve? On Shark Tank, the investors reject "a solution in search of a problem": a product nobody actually needs.
  3. What are the possible solutions? They must be feasible, not unrealistic.
  4. What exactly are the inputs and outputs?
  5. What issues or difficulties may arise?
  6. What is the budget and deadline? Either the client proposes them, or they come through a tender process.

4.3Requirement Engineering Steps: Gathering, Analysis, Documentation, Review

🎓 From the lecture Gathering → Analysis → Documentation → Review, ending in the final SRS in a standard format.
  • Gathering (elicitation): collect requirements from the client and stakeholders (techniques in 4.4–4.7).
  • Analysis: remove conflicts, inconsistencies and incompleteness, using the tools in chapter 5.
  • Documentation: write the SRS (chapter 6).
  • Review: a final check before sign-off.
➕ Added for exam Pressman splits requirement engineering into seven tasks: Inception (basic understanding, stakeholders), Elicitation (gathering), Elaboration (refining into analysis models), Negotiation (resolving conflicts and priorities), Specification (writing the SRS or models), Validation (checking for errors, ambiguity and omissions) and Requirements management (tracking changes, often with a traceability matrix that links each requirement to its design, code and tests). Sommerville's version has four: feasibility study, elicitation and analysis, specification, validation.

4.4Interview and Group Discussion

🎓 From the lecture

Interview / meeting

One-on-one or in pairs. Let the client say everything first, then ask questions to clear up confusion.

Group discussion

Like a placement GD: both sides take part in groups, with cross-questioning and debate. The drawback is that extroverts dominate and introverts may not speak up, so some views are lost.
➕ Added for exam Closed (structured) interviews follow a fixed set of questions; open (unstructured) interviews have no fixed agenda. Brainstorming is a group session where ideas are listed without criticism and filtered later. Other elicitation techniques: questionnaires/surveys (reach many users cheaply), observation/ethnography (watch users work in their real environment), document analysis (study existing forms, manuals and reports) and prototyping (chapter 2).

4.5Delphi Technique (Anonymous Written Iterations)

🎓 From the lecture The Delphi technique fixes the group-discussion problem with a written, anonymous, iterative group discussion:
  1. Experts or stakeholders each write their requirements, or answers to a questionnaire, anonymously, so everyone can express freely without revealing who they are.
  2. The sheets are rotated; each person reads others' answers and updates them.
  3. This repeats over several iterations.
  4. The results are summed up to find the majority view (consensus).
➕ Added for exam Delphi was developed at the RAND Corporation in the 1950s for forecasting. A coordinator/facilitator collects and summarises each round. Its key features: anonymity, iteration with controlled feedback, and a statistical group response. The same technique appears again in chapter 10 for expert-judgement cost estimation, where "Wideband Delphi" adds group discussion between rounds.

4.6Facilitated Workshop and Quality Function Deployment (QFD)

🎓 From the lecture

Facilitated workshop

Collaborative, creative team sessions where the client and the developers explore market trends and new ideas together.

Quality Function Deployment (QFD)

Prioritises requirements into very important (core) → important → not so important, so the team builds what matters most to the customer first.
➕ Added for exam
  • A facilitated workshop is known as JAD (Joint Application Development), from IBM, or FAST (Facilitated Application Specification Technique). A neutral facilitator runs the meeting, with preset rules and an agenda, and stakeholders and developers jointly produce the requirements.
  • QFD, from Japan in the 1970s, translates customer needs into technical requirements. It classifies requirements into three types:
    • Normal requirements: stated explicitly by the customer. Satisfaction grows with how well they are met.
    • Expected requirements: so basic the customer doesn't mention them, but their absence causes great dissatisfaction (easy installation, reliability).
    • Exciting requirements: go beyond expectations and delight the customer (an unexpected useful feature).
  • QFD uses a "House of Quality" matrix to map customer needs to technical features.
🎯 IBPS exam alert "Requirements that the customer does not state but assumes are present" → Expected requirements (QFD). "JAD / FAST" → a facilitated requirement-gathering meeting.

4.7Role Play and Use Case Approach

🎓 From the lecture

Role play of different user types

Team members act as different kinds of users, for example a rich user and a middle-class user. Each has a different mindset and interacts with a hypothetical prototype differently, which helps refine and filter requirements.

Use case approach

A visual representation of the actors (users or external systems), the use cases (things the system does for them) and their interactions.
➕ Added for exam
  • Use case diagrams are part of UML and were introduced by Ivar Jacobson.
  • Notation:
    • Actor: stick figure, outside the system.
    • Use case: ellipse.
    • System boundary: rectangle.
    • Association: a line between an actor and a use case.
  • «include»: the base use case always uses the included one. Every withdrawal needs authentication.
  • «extend»: the extending use case adds optional behaviour under a condition. Printing a receipt only if asked.
  • Actors can be primary (they start the use case) or secondary (they support it, like the bank server).
⚠️ Exam trap In «include» the arrow points from the base use case to the included one; in «extend» it points from the extending use case to the base. "Mandatory" = include, "optional" = extend.

4.8Feasibility Study and Types of RequirementsADDED

➕ Added for exam The lecture mentions checking feasibility in the planning phase. Its types are remembered as TELOS:
TypeQuestion
TechnicalDo we have the technology, hardware and skills?
Economic (cost–benefit)Do the benefits exceed the costs?
LegalDoes it comply with laws (data protection, licensing)?
OperationalWill users actually use it, and does it fit the organisation?
ScheduleCan it be finished in the required time?
Requirement levels: user requirements are high-level statements in natural language for the customer; system requirements are detailed, precise descriptions for developers. Requirements are also split into functional (what the system does) and non-functional (how well it does it: performance, security, usability). Chapter 6 covers these in detail.

4.9Solved Exam QuestionsADDED

Q1Which requirement-gathering technique uses anonymous, iterative rounds of written responses from experts?
Answer: the Delphi technique.
Q2JAD stands for:
Answer: Joint Application Development, a facilitated workshop with users and developers.
Q3In QFD, requirements the customer does not mention but would be very dissatisfied without are: (a) Normal (b) Expected (c) Exciting (d) Derived
Answer: (b) Expected.
Q4In a use case diagram, optional behaviour added to a base use case under a condition is shown with:
Answer: «extend». Mandatory, always-used behaviour is «include».
Q5Which feasibility study checks whether the benefits of a system outweigh its costs?
Answer: Economic feasibility (cost–benefit analysis).
Q6The main output of the requirement engineering process is:
Answer: the SRS (Software Requirement Specification).

  • Techniques: interview · group discussion (extroverts dominate) · Delphi (anonymous, written, iterative, RAND) · facilitated workshop (JAD/FAST) · QFD (normal, expected, exciting) · role play · use cases.
  • Use case: actor (stick figure), use case (ellipse), system boundary (box); «include» = mandatory, «extend» = optional.
