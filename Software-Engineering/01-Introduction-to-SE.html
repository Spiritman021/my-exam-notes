Chapter 1

Introduction to Software Engineering

Writing a program that works on your laptop is programming. Delivering software that a paying client asked for, on a budget and a deadline, that keeps working for years while the client keeps changing their mind, is software engineering. This chapter defines the discipline, explains why software behaves differently from physical products, and lists the false beliefs, layers, challenges and quality goals that the rest of the subject builds on.

1.1Meaning of Software Engineering (Building a House)

🎓 From the lecture Break the name, understand each half, then join it back.
  • Software brings to mind programs, a set of instructions and some documentation.
  • Engineering brings to mind designing, creating and building something that solves an end user's real problem in a systematic, disciplined, procedural manner.
Joined: design and build a software product that solves the end user's problem in a systematic, disciplined way. You never jump straight to coding; planning, requirement gathering, development, testing and maintenance are all involved.

Example: building a house

  • You tell the builder "start construction on my plot". He does not turn up with cement the next day. First he asks what you want: how many floors, a big hall or a big kitchen, attached or common bathrooms, an L-shaped kitchen, how much garden. These are the user requirements, and the work is customised to them.
  • Once requirements are known he can give a time estimate (about 1–1.5 years) and a budget, usually as an upper bound ("keep 20–25 lakh ready"). Work starts only when both sides agree.
  • An architect draws the blueprint (rooms, doors arranged per Vastu): the design.
  • Material and labour arrive and construction follows steps: base (its width depends on the soil), pillars, walls.
  • Mid-way you visit and ask for a change: "make the gallery bigger so my parents can hang a swing, even if the garden shrinks". Customised change requests happen in software too.
Software engineering: the application of a systematic, disciplined, quantifiable approach to the development, operation and maintenance of software (IEEE definition). The goal is software that is reliable, efficient, delivered on time and within budget, and meets the user's needs.
➕ Added for exam
  • The term "software engineering" was popularised at the NATO Software Engineering Conference of 1968 (Garmisch, Germany), called to address the software crisis (see 1.5).
  • Program vs software product: a program is code written by one person for personal use, often undocumented. A software product is used by many people, developed by a team and comes with documentation, manuals and support. Software = programs + documentation + operating procedures.
  • Generic vs custom (bespoke) software: generic products (MS Office, a browser) are built for the open market; custom software is built for one client's specification (a bank's loan system).

1.2Characteristics of Software

🎓 From the lecture
  1. It is engineered, not manufactured. The proof is in the name: it is software engineering, not software manufacturing. Cars and smartphones are manufactured: raw material is combined by machinery component by component, then finished and shipped. Software is engineered: designed, developed and tested through phases.
  2. It does not wear out, but it deteriorates. A car, phone, laptop or mic wears out physically over time. Software does not. It degrades because the client's heavy, repeated changes pull it away from its original motive. After enough changes "its soul (aatma) changes": the core functionality becomes something else.
  3. It is custom-built. In a mall you don't buy a shirt because it looked good on someone else; you try it in the trial room to fit your body, colour and personality. Or you give cloth to a tailor who stitches to your measurements. Software too is built to this user's requirements, which is why the first step is always asking for them.
  4. It cannot be touched. Software is intangible, unlike a mobile, a car or any hardware.
➕ Added for exam This is Pressman's classic pair of curves. Hardware follows the bathtub curve: high early failures (manufacturing defects), a low steady period, then rising failures as parts wear out. Software ideally has early failures (bugs) that fall and then stay flat, because nothing physical ages. In practice each change introduces new defects, causing a spike, and the minimum failure rate creeps upward. That upward drift is deterioration. Two more listed characteristics: most software is still custom-built rather than assembled from existing components (though component reuse is growing), and software is flexible (easy to change, which is both its strength and the cause of deterioration).
⚠️ Exam trap "Software wears out with time" is false. "Software deteriorates due to change" is true. Also, a software defect is a design (engineering) error; there are no "spare parts" for software, so maintenance means changing the design, not replacing a part.

1.3Software Myths: Management, Customer and Practitioner

🎓 From the lecture A myth is a false belief: incorrect information that settles in our heads and creates confusion. Think of the Chinese-whispers game: the first person whispers a message, each person in the line changes it a little, and the last person hears something completely different. Over time, wrong interpretations of how software is built have spread the same way. The lecture gives a reality check for each group.
GroupMythReality (lecture's example)
Management"We already have all the standards and procedures needed for development."Overconfidence. Software is custom-built, so new requirements may not be served efficiently by old standards and procedures.
"Buying the latest hardware will improve development."Latest is not the same as right. A father buys his child the newest phone with a great camera and storage, but the child wanted it for gaming and gaming runs badly, so the child is not happy. Requirements come first.
"If we are behind schedule, add more programmers to catch up."It makes things worse. Existing developers know the progress, conditions and constraints; newcomers must be trained and guided, which wastes their time and delays the project further.
Customer"A general statement of objectives is enough to start writing programs; details can be filled in later."Like shooting a film from a generic plot (hero, villain, happy ending) without a detailed script. Vague requirements lead to the wrong product.
"Requirements change all the time, but software is flexible, so changes are easy."Changes are cheap early, during requirements, and very costly after design and coding, because work must be redone.
Practitioner (developer)"Once the program is written and works, our job is done."Roughly 50–60% of the total effort is spent after delivery, in maintenance.
"Until the program runs, I can't assess its quality."Quality can be checked phase by phase (reviews of requirements and design, using tools) long before execution.
"The only deliverable is the working program."A bicycle is not just two wheels that turn; it needs gears, brakes, tyres and a design. Deliverables include the SRS, design documents, test plans and user manuals.
"SE makes us write unnecessary documentation and slows us down."Documentation is essential for maintenance, re-engineering and versioning. The rule is to write the necessary documentation, not volumes of it.
➕ Added for exam The "add more programmers" myth is Brooks' Law, from Fred Brooks' The Mythical Man-Month (1975): "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later." Two reasons: ramp-up time for newcomers, and communication paths growing as n(n − 1)/2. Going from 4 to 8 people raises communication paths from 6 to 28. Studies (Boehm) estimate that fixing a defect after release can cost up to 100 times more than fixing it during requirements.
🎯 IBPS exam alert Questions take the form "Which of the following is a management myth?" Memorise the grouping: standards / latest hardware / add people = management; vague objectives / easy changes = customer; job done when it runs / quality only after running / only deliverable is the program / documentation slows us = practitioner.

1.4Software Engineering as a Layered Technology

🎓 From the lecture SE is described as four layers, each supporting the one above:
  • Quality focus: the commitment to quality (usability, maintainability, security and the other attributes in 1.6) that every layer must serve.
  • Process: the foundation layer. It holds the framework of activities: communication, planning, modeling (analysis and design), construction (coding and testing), deployment and maintenance.
  • Methods: the technical "how-to" for each activity: how to gather requirements, how to design, how to test.
  • Tools: automated or semi-automated support for the process and the methods.
⚠️ Exam trap In Pressman's wording, the quality focus is the bedrock (bottom-most layer) and the process layer is the foundation of software engineering. Both words appear in options. If asked "Which layer is the foundation?", answer Process. If asked "Which layer is at the bottom / the bedrock?", answer Quality focus. From bottom to top: Quality → Process → Methods → Tools.
➕ Added for exam Tools that are integrated so that information created by one tool can be used by another form CASE (Computer-Aided Software Engineering). Upper CASE tools support early phases (requirements, design diagrams); lower CASE tools support later phases (code generation, testing, debugging).

1.5Problems and Challenges in Software Development

🎓 From the lecture
  • Unclear requirements: incomplete, or ambiguous (one statement with two meanings). The team needs extra brainstorming sessions just to work out what the client means.
  • Tight time and budget: clients set unrealistic expectations and want everything fast and cheap. As the saying goes, "haste is the devil's work" (jaldi ka kaam shaitan ka).
  • Skill gaps and human error within the development team.
  • Changing requirements: clients keep asking for changes, and late changes are hard and costly (see the myth in 1.3).
➕ Added for exam Software crisis is the name given (1960s–70s) to these problems at industry scale: projects running over budget and over time, software that was inefficient, of low quality, did not meet requirements, was hard to maintain, and sometimes was never delivered. Causes include growing size and complexity, poor project management, lack of training, and hardware prices falling while software costs rose. Software engineering, with its systematic process, was the response.

1.6Software Quality Attributes

🎓 From the lecture
AttributeMeaning
CorrectnessDoes exactly what the requirements say.
UsabilityUser-friendly; easy to learn and operate.
ReliabilityTrustworthy; performs consistently and recovers quickly from failure.
EfficiencyMakes the best use of resources (CPU, memory, time).
MaintainabilityEasy to fix, update and enhance.
ScalabilityScales up and down as load changes.
PortabilityRuns across different environments and platforms.
SecurityProtects against passive and active attacks and unauthorised access.
ReusabilityParts can be reused elsewhere, saving time, effort and cost. Like reusing pages of a senior's assignment.
ModularityDivided into independent modules. Measured by coupling and cohesion, covered in chapter 9.
➕ Added for exam McCall's quality model (1977) groups 11 factors under three perspectives:
PerspectiveQuestion it answersFactors
Product operationHow well does it run?Correctness, Reliability, Efficiency, Integrity (security), Usability
Product revisionHow easy is it to change?Maintainability, Flexibility, Testability
Product transitionHow easy is it to move or adapt?Portability, Reusability, Interoperability
ISO/IEC 9126 names six characteristics: Functionality, Reliability, Usability, Efficiency, Maintainability, Portability (mnemonic FRUEMP). It was replaced by ISO/IEC 25010, which adds Security and Compatibility for eight in total.
⚠️ Exam trap In McCall's model, Portability and Reusability belong to product transition, not revision; Maintainability, Flexibility and Testability belong to revision. "Integrity" in McCall means security (controlling unauthorised access), not data integrity.

1.7Solved Exam QuestionsADDED

Q1Software is said to "deteriorate" rather than "wear out". The main reason is: (a) physical ageing of media (b) side effects of changes made during maintenance (c) virus attacks (d) hardware failure
Answer: (b). Every change can introduce new defects, so the failure rate drifts upward.
Q2"Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later" is known as:
Answer: Brooks' Law. It is the reality behind the management myth of adding programmers.
Q3Which layer of software engineering is described as its foundation? (a) Tools (b) Methods (c) Process (d) Quality
Answer: (c) Process. The quality focus is the bedrock beneath it.
Q4"A general statement of objectives is sufficient to begin writing programs." This is a:
Answer: Customer myth.
Q5In McCall's model, which factor belongs to product transition? (a) Testability (b) Portability (c) Correctness (d) Flexibility
Answer: (b) Portability. The transition factors are portability, reusability and interoperability.
Q6The term "software engineering" was first popularised at:
Answer: the 1968 NATO conference held in response to the software crisis.

⚡ Rapid Revision

  • SE = a systematic, disciplined, quantifiable approach to developing, operating and maintaining software. Lecture analogy: building a house (requirements → time and budget → blueprint → construction → change requests).
  • Software is engineered, not manufactured; it does not wear out but deteriorates because of changes; it is custom-built; it is intangible.
  • Hardware failure = bathtub curve. Software = falling then flat (idealised), with spikes and an upward drift on every change (actual).
  • Myths: management (standards, latest hardware, add people → Brooks' Law), customer (vague objectives, easy change), practitioner (done when it runs, quality only after running, program is the only deliverable, documentation slows us).
  • Layers from bottom to top: Quality focus (bedrock) → Process (foundation) → Methods (how-to) → Tools (CASE).
  • Software crisis: over budget, late, low quality, unmaintainable. It led to the 1968 NATO conference and the term SE.
  • McCall: operation (correctness, reliability, efficiency, integrity, usability) · revision (maintainability, flexibility, testability) · transition (portability, reusability, interoperability).
  • ISO 9126: FRUEMP. Replaced by ISO 25010, which adds security and compatibility.
