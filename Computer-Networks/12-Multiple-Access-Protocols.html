Chapter 12

Multiple Access Protocols

Many stations share one channel (a bus cable or the air). If two transmit at the same time, their signals collide and both frames are destroyed. The MAC sublayer (Chapter 11) runs a multiple access protocol to decide who may send, and when. The lecture builds them in order of cleverness: pure ALOHA → slotted ALOHA → CSMA → CSMA/CD and CSMA/CA.

12.0Why Multiple Access? Classification ADDED

🎓 From the lecture Many hosts are connected multipoint to one shared cable / link / channel. A host must access the shared channel to transmit. If another host puts its data on the link at the same moment, there is a collision. The goal of access control: keep collisions as few as possible. Spoiler numbers: pure ALOHA ≈ 18% efficiency, slotted ALOHA ≈ 37% ("double").
  • Random access (contention): no station is superior; anyone may send when it wants, and collisions are possible. All of this chapter.
  • Controlled access: stations agree on who sends. Reservation (book a slot first), polling (a primary asks each secondary in turn: poll/select), token passing (only the holder of the token sends: Token Ring, FDDI).
  • Channelization: the bandwidth is split. FDMA by frequency bands, TDMA by time slots (GSM), CDMA by codes (all send at once with orthogonal codes).

12.1Pure ALOHA

Pure ALOHA: the simplest random-access protocol. A station sends whenever it has a frame, without checking the channel. If no acknowledgement comes back, it assumes a collision, waits a random back-off time, and resends. Developed at the University of Hawaii (ALOHAnet, 1970, Norman Abramson) ADDED.
🎓 From the lecture — the flowchart
  1. Start: set the back-off counter K = 0 (attempts so far). Also fix a maximum limit (example: 5).
  2. Send the frame on the shared link.
  3. Wait 2 × Tp: one Tp for the data to reach the receiver, one Tp for the acknowledgement ("haan bhaiya, aapki chitthi mil gayi") to come back. Have patience for that long.
  4. ACK received? Yes → success. No → it was a collision (the receiver won't acknowledge a letter it never got).
  5. On a collision: K = K + 1. Has K reached the limit? Yes → abort ("take a pause, come back later and try"). No → wait the back-off time (12.2) and resend.

12.2Back-off Waiting Time (Binary Exponential Backoff)

🎓 From the lecture
  • Waiting time TB = R × Tp. R (sometimes written t) is a random number between 0 and 2ᴷ − 1, where K = the current back-off value (number of collisions so far).
  • The range grows with each collision: K = 1 → 0 to 1; K = 2 → 0 to 3; K = 3 → 0 to 7; K = 4 → 0 to 15.
  • Why grow the range? With a small range there are few choices, so two stations may pick the same R, finish waiting at the same moment, transmit together and collide again. With a bigger range (0–15), one picks 7 and the other 3: different waits, so they don't send together. Chances of collision decrease (not to zero).
  • If collisions keep happening until K reaches the limit: "bhai, tum ruk jao", you are spoiling your own and everyone's work. Abort and give the others a chance.

➕ Why "binary exponential"? The range doubles (×2, binary) after every collision (exponential growth: 2ᴷ). It adapts to load: light traffic means short waits; heavy traffic quickly spreads stations apart. In textbooks (Forouzan) Kmax = 15, and TB may use Tp or the frame time Tfr as the unit. Ethernet uses a truncated version: the range stops growing after K = 10 (0 – 1023 slots), the station gives up after 16 attempts, and the slot time = 51.2 µs (512 bit times at 10 Mbps).
⚠️ Exam trap The lecture's limit of 5 is only an example; the standard answer is Kmax = 15. The range is 0 to 2ᴷ − 1 (not 1 to 2ᴷ). After the 1st collision, R ∈ {0, 1}, so a station may retransmit immediately (R = 0).

12.3Vulnerable Time in Pure ALOHA

Vulnerable time: the time period during which, if another station starts sending, there is a possibility of collision with our frame. For pure ALOHA it is 2 × Tt.
🎓 From the lecture Stations 1, 2 and 3 send frames. Station 1's tail collides with station 2's head; station 3's head collides with station 2's tail. To avoid collision, the earlier frame must move back and the later one forward. If A starts at t1, nobody else may start in the Tt before it or the Tt after it. Each frame takes the same Tt, so the collision-prone period is Tt + Tt = 2Tt. Even slightly early or late causes a collision. This happens because access is completely random: no rule says who goes when. That randomness gives only ≈ 18% efficiency.
Throughput formula (added)
G = average number of frames generated per frame time Tt (offered load) Pure ALOHA throughput S = G · e^(−2G) Maximum S_max = 1/(2e) ≈ 0.184 = 18.4% at G = 0.5
Q. Pure ALOHA, 200-bit frames on a shared 200 kbps channel. (a) Vulnerable time? (b) Throughput when the system produces 1000, 500 and 250 frames/s?
Tt = 200 / 200 000 = 1 ms, so vulnerable time = 2 × 1 = 2 ms.
1000 frames/s → G = 1000 × 1 ms = 1 → S = 1 × e⁻² = 0.135 → 135 frames/s succeed.
500 frames/s → G = 0.5 → S = 0.5 × e⁻¹ = 0.184 → 92 frames/s (the maximum %).
250 frames/s → G = 0.25 → S = 0.25 × e⁻⁰·⁵ = 0.152 → 38 frames/s.

12.4Slotted ALOHA

Slotted ALOHA: time is divided into slots of length Tt (one frame time). A station may send only at the beginning of a slot. A frame ready mid-slot waits for the next slot. Clocks must be synchronised.
🎓 From the lecture Make one rule in pure ALOHA: transmit only at the beginning of a time slot; not slightly after it (the tail would collide), not slightly before it. Slotted ALOHA "listened" to this: the timeline is divided into slots, each slot = Tt. Frames now fit exactly into slots: one ends where the next begins, with no partial overlaps.
Why only 37% then? If station 4 also sends at the start of the same slot, it is following the rule, but the two frames now overlap completely ("body on body"). So collisions remain, but the vulnerable time is halved to Tt, and the efficiency is doubled to ≈ 37%.
Throughput formula (added)
Slotted ALOHA throughput S = G · e^(−G) Maximum S_max = 1/e ≈ 0.368 = 36.8% at G = 1
Same Q, slotted (200-bit frames, 200 kbps, Tt = 1 ms). Vulnerable time = 1 ms.
G = 1 → S = e⁻¹ = 0.368 → 368 frames/s. G = 0.5 → S = 0.5 × e⁻⁰·⁵ = 0.303 → 151 frames/s. G = 0.25 → S = 0.25 × e⁻⁰·²⁵ = 0.195 → 49 frames/s.
Q. A 1 Mbps channel's maximum useful throughput? Pure: 0.184 × 1 Mbps ≈ 184 kbps. Slotted: ≈ 368 kbps.
⚠️ Exam trap Slotted's maximum is exactly double pure's (1/e vs 1/2e), because its vulnerable time is exactly half. The peaks occur at different loads: pure at G = 0.5, slotted at G = 1. Slotting removes partial collisions, not full ones.

12.5CSMA (Carrier Sense Multiple Access)

CSMA: "sense before transmit" (listen before talk). Each station senses the carrier (the shared channel) and transmits only if it is idle. If it is busy, it does not transmit.
🎓 From the lecture — the door peephole When someone knocks, do you open the door straight away, or look through the peephole (doorbin) first? You look first. CSMA does the same: each station checks the link at the point where it is connected. A wave on the link means busy, so don't send (you would spoil the other person's data and your own). No wave means idle, so send.
The biggest problem: it checks only at its own point. A signal still travelling towards it is invisible, so collisions are reduced, not eliminated. Hence the detection (CD) and avoidance (CA) variants.

12.6Vulnerable Time in CSMA

In CSMA, collisions happen because of propagation delay. Vulnerable time = Tp: the time for a signal to travel from one end of the medium to the other.
🎓 From the lecture — the dogs in the passage Flats 1, 2, 3 and 4 open onto a common passage (the shared link) that ends at a dog house. Flat 1 keeps ~10 dogs; every day the queue of dogs walks from Flat 1 (source) to the dog house (destination). Others fear being bitten (big injections!), so before stepping out they check through the peephole (carrier sensing).
  • Flat 3 sees the dogs, so stays in. Fine.
  • Flat 2 looks before the queue has reached its door, sees nothing, opens the door, and gets bitten: a collision.
  • The whole period while the queue is travelling from source to destination is vulnerable. Once all the dogs reach the dog house (propagation over), there is no danger. Hence vulnerable time = Tp.
  • The bitten person screams, and that scream travels along the passage ("don't come out, dogs!"). That is the collision signal, which propagates over the carrier and later helps stations detect that their own data was involved (CSMA/CD).
🎯 IBPS exam alert — vulnerable time Pure ALOHA = 2 × Tt · Slotted ALOHA = Tt · CSMA = Tp. ALOHA's depends on frame (transmission) time; CSMA's on propagation time. A smaller Tp (shorter cable) means fewer CSMA collisions.

12.7CSMA Persistence Methods

🎓 From the lecture All stations want to transmit as soon as possible, so each senses the carrier and pounces when it is idle. If all pounce together, they collide. Persistence methods answer: how to sense (continuously or at random times), and what to do when the channel is found idle or busy. Three types: 1-persistent, non-persistent, p-persistent.

12.81-Persistent Method

🎓 From the lecture "Sense continuously": keep your eye glued to the peephole, never move away. When the carrier becomes empty, transmit immediately. Problem: every station is "bhara pada" (loaded and waiting), so the moment the carrier goes idle, all of them jump in together, causing a collision. So collision chances are highest here.
➕ Facts "1" = it transmits with probability 1 when idle. Advantage: no idle time is wasted (good when the load is light). Used by classic Ethernet (CSMA/CD).

12.9Non-Persistent Method

🎓 From the lecture "Don't sense continuously." Look through the peephole; if it is busy, go and sit comfortably on the sofa, come back after a random time and check again. If idle, transmit; if busy, don't (basic etiquette). Two problems remain:
  1. Collision still possible: station A and station C may both pick the same random time (say 5 minutes), both find the link idle after 5 minutes, both send, and collide.
  2. Channel wasted: A picks 5 minutes, but the channel became idle just after it left. For those 5 minutes the link sits totally empty ("curfew laga hua hai") while A had data to send. The resource is not optimally utilised.

12.10P-Persistent Method

🎓 From the lecture An "intelligent approach": p stands for probability. Each station connected to the carrier is given a fixed probability, for example A: p = 0.9, B: 0.6, C: 0.3. A holds the highest, then B, then C.

12.11P-Persistent CSMA

🎓 From the lecture — "mauke pe chauka, but the ball must be good" Stations sense continuously (like 1-persistent), ready to hit a four when the chance comes. But when the carrier is found idle, they do not all jump together. Transmission happens according to probability: the station with the highest probability (A) goes first, then B, then C. Following this pattern greatly reduces the collision rate.
⚠️ Exam trap — the textbook definition differs The lecture's "each station has its own fixed probability, highest goes first" is a simplified picture. The standard (Forouzan / Tanenbaum) algorithm, used on slotted channels, is: when the channel is idle, a station transmits with probability p, and with probability q = 1 − p it waits for the next slot and senses again. If the channel is busy then, it behaves as if a collision occurred and uses back-off. Stations normally share the same p; the randomness, not a fixed ranking, spreads them out. It combines the advantages of 1-persistent (no idle waste) and non-persistent (fewer collisions).

12.12CSMA/CD (Collision Detection)

CSMA/CD: CSMA plus collision detection. A station keeps monitoring the line while it transmits. If it detects a collision, it stops at once, sends a jam signal, and retries after a binary exponential back-off. It works only on wired (guided) media. Standard: IEEE 802.3 (Ethernet).
🎓 From the lecture
  • Carrier sensing and multiple access stay as before; the new part is collision detection: can a station find out that its own data was involved in a collision? Yes, but only on wired media (very important point).
  • Stations A, B, C and D share a channel. A and D transmit at about the same time, so a collision happens. Immediately "mohalle mein halla" (an uproar in the neighbourhood): the collision signal propagates through the whole channel.
  • Everyone learns that a collision happened, but whose data was in it? Key idea: when the collision signal reaches A, if A still has some bits left to transmit (the frame isn't finished), then A knows "101% my data was involved". The same goes for D.
  • This works only when the transmission time is long enough; see 12.13.
➕ More facts
  • Detection is by energy level on the line: zero (idle), normal (one sender), abnormal / double (collision).
  • Jam signal: a short 32-bit pattern (IEEE 802.3; some texts say 48 bits) that makes sure every station notices the collision.
  • Unlike ALOHA, there are no ACKs for collision handling: the sender itself knows. Wasted time is limited to the moment of detection, not the whole frame.
  • Efficiency η = 1 / (1 + 6.44a), where a = Tp / Tt. A small a (long frames, short cable) gives high efficiency.
  • Modern switched, full-duplex Ethernet has no collisions, so CSMA/CD is not used there; it matters for hubs and shared half-duplex links.

12.13CSMA/CD: Minimum Frame Length (Tt ≥ 2Tp)

🎓 From the lecture
  • If the frame is too short, the sender has finished before the collision signal returns, so it can't tell whose data collided.
  • Tt ∝ length, so we need a long enough frame. How long, minimum?
  • Worst case: A sends to D. A's data has almost reached D (one second away). D looks through its peephole, sees nothing yet, transmits, and the collision happens at D's end. A's data took Tp to get there; the collision signal takes another Tp to come back to A. Total = 2Tp.
  • So A must still be transmitting for at least 2Tp: Tt ≥ 2Tp (equal is the minimum; greater is even better).
  • Putting Tt = L/B and Tp = d/v: L ≥ 2 × Tp × B. With a smaller length you can never detect it.
Essential formulas
Condition Tt ≥ 2 · Tp Minimum frame L_min = 2 · Tp · B = 2 · (d / v) · B Max cable length d_max = (L · v) / (2 · B) Efficiency η = 1 / (1 + 6.44 a), a = Tp / Tt
Numericals (added) Q1 (classic Ethernet). 10 Mbps, maximum Tp = 25.6 µs. L_min = 2 × 25.6×10⁻⁶ × 10⁷ = 512 bits = 64 bytes. This is exactly why the minimum Ethernet frame is 64 bytes and the slot time is 51.2 µs.
Q2. 1 km cable, v = 2×10⁸ m/s, 100 Mbps. Tp = 10³ / 2×10⁸ = 5 µs. L_min = 2 × 5×10⁻⁶ × 10⁸ = 1000 bits = 125 bytes.
Q3. Frames of 1000 bits at 1 Gbps, v = 2×10⁸ m/s. Max length? Tt = 1 µs ≥ 2Tp → Tp ≤ 0.5 µs → d ≤ 0.5×10⁻⁶ × 2×10⁸ = 100 m. Higher speed means a shorter allowed cable (or a larger minimum frame: Gigabit Ethernet uses carrier extension to 512 bytes).
Q4. a = 0.1 → η = 1 / (1 + 0.644) = 0.608 ≈ 60.8%.
⚠️ Exam trap The factor is 2Tp (round trip), not Tp. If the bandwidth doubles while the cable stays the same, the minimum frame size doubles. Ethernet frames shorter than 64 bytes are "runts" and are padded up to 64.

12.14CSMA/CA (Collision Avoidance)

CSMA/CA: used on wireless networks, where collisions cannot be detected reliably, so they are avoided using waiting: inter-frame space (IFS), a random contention window, positive acknowledgements, and optionally RTS/CTS. Standard: IEEE 802.11 (Wi-Fi).
🎓 From the lecture CD worked on wired media, where a station can detect the collision signal while still transmitting. In wireless, everything is unguided, "khuli hawa, full freedom", and the signal strength keeps dropping, so how do you detect? You don't; you avoid. Collisions can still happen, but chances are lower, because a lot of waiting is introduced. Waiting is the key factor.
Example: two elders at home have had a fight. If they face each other, there will be another clash (collision). So the family keeps them apart: "you wait until he leaves the house; then he waits while you go".
➕ Why CD fails in wireless
  1. A radio can't listen while transmitting (its own strong signal drowns any weak incoming one; half-duplex).
  2. Signals fade with distance, so a colliding signal may be too weak to notice.
  3. Hidden station problem: two stations may be out of each other's range (Figure 12.12).

12.15CSMA/CA: IFS, Contention Window and Acknowledgement

🎓 From the lecture — three steps
  1. IFS (Inter-Frame Space): host A senses continuously; the channel is busy, busy, busy, then becomes idle. A wants to send immediately, but: "ruk, bhai!" Others are also waiting and would collide. First wait a fixed IFS (nanoseconds, microseconds, …). It may differ per station by priority: a high-priority station gets a shorter IFS.
  2. Contention window: even after IFS, don't send yet. The window is a period divided into slots; pick a random number (from the same 2ᴷ back-off range, growing exponentially) and wait that many extra slots.
  3. Acknowledgement: after sending, if the ACK arrives in time, the transmission was successful. If no ACK arrives within the time, assume a collision ("itna wait karne ke baad bhi kaam nahi hua").
➕ What the lecture didn't name
  • IFS types (shortest = highest priority): SIFS (Short IFS: before CTS, data after CTS, and ACK) < PIFS (Point coordination IFS) < DIFS (Distributed IFS: before starting to contend) < EIFS.
  • Contention window: the window size doubles after each failure (binary exponential back-off). The back-off timer freezes while the channel is busy and resumes when it is idle (it does not restart).
  • NAV (Network Allocation Vector): RTS and CTS carry a duration. Every other station sets its NAV timer and stays silent until it expires. This is "virtual carrier sensing".
  • Wi-Fi acknowledges every unicast frame, because a sender can't otherwise know about a collision.

12.16CSMA/CA Flowchart with RTS/CTS

🎓 From the lecture — "lives in a game"
  1. K = 0 initially, with a limit (example: 5 "lives"; at 0 none have been used yet, "ek dum fresh").
  2. Sense continuously; not idle → keep checking. Idle → wait IFS.
  3. Pick R from 0 to 2ᴷ − 1 and wait R slots (contention window). At K = 0, the range is just {0}.
  4. Send RTS to the central admin unit (the access point): "I've crossed every waiting rule and come from far; now send me a CTS". Start a timer.
  5. No CTS (Clear To Send) before the timer ends → treat as a collision (go to step 8).
  6. CTS received → still wait one more IFS ("last time wait", in case someone is still transmitting) → send the frame.
  7. Wait for the ACK. ACK in time → "Success, my tapasya paid off".
  8. No ACK (or no CTS) → K = K + 1 (one life lost). K > limit → abort ("game over: too much traffic; come back much later"). Otherwise wait the back-off time TB = R × slot (≈ round-trip time) and start sensing again.
The lecture notes that the "shared link" drawing only shows the sequence (sense → IFS → CW → ACK); real CSMA/CA runs over wireless, with hosts A, B, C around an access point.
⚠️ Exam trap RTS = Request To Send (the lecture says "Ready to Send"; the standard expansion is Request). CTS = Clear To Send. The lecture also says a range for K = 3 of "0 to 8"; the correct range is 0 to 7 (2³ − 1 = 7). RTS/CTS solves the hidden station problem; it does not fully solve the exposed station problem (a station needlessly stays silent because it overhears a transmission that wouldn't actually interfere).
🎯 IBPS exam alert "Ethernet uses?" → CSMA/CD (802.3). "Wi-Fi uses?" → CSMA/CA (802.11). "Collision detection is not possible in?" → wireless. "Hidden terminal problem is solved by?" → RTS/CTS. "Shortest IFS / highest priority?" → SIFS. "Minimum Ethernet frame?" → 64 bytes. "Max efficiency of slotted ALOHA?" → 36.8% (1/e).

⚡ Rapid Revision — Chapter 12

  • Shared channel → collisions → the MAC needs access control. Random access (ALOHA, CSMA family) · controlled (reservation, polling, token) · channelization (FDMA, TDMA, CDMA).
  • Pure ALOHA: send anytime; wait 2Tp for the ACK; no ACK → K + 1 → K > Kmax? abort : wait R × Tp, R ∈ [0, 2ᴷ − 1]. Vulnerable 2Tt; S = Ge⁻²ᴳ; max 18.4% at G = 0.5.
  • Binary exponential back-off: the range doubles per collision (0–1, 0–3, 0–7, 0–15), so collision chances fall. Kmax = 15; Ethernet caps the range at K = 10 and gives up after 16 attempts.
  • Slotted ALOHA: send only at the slot start (slot = Tt). Vulnerable Tt; S = Ge⁻ᴳ; max 36.8% at G = 1; full overlaps still possible.
  • CSMA (peephole): sense, then send. Vulnerable time = Tp (the dogs in the passage); collisions come from propagation delay; the "scream" is the collision signal.
  • 1-persistent: sense continuously, send at once → most collisions (Ethernet). Non-persistent: busy → random wait → fewer collisions but an idle channel is wasted. p-persistent: idle → send with prob p, else defer one slot (lecture: highest-probability station first).
  • CSMA/CD (wired, 802.3): monitor while sending; collision → stop, jam, back-off. Still sending when the collision signal returns → "my data". Need Tt ≥ 2Tp → L ≥ 2·Tp·B. 10 Mbps, Tp 25.6 µs → 64 bytes. η = 1/(1 + 6.44a).
  • CSMA/CA (wireless, 802.11): can't detect, so avoid by waiting. IFS (priority; SIFS < PIFS < DIFS) → contention window (random slots) → RTS → CTS → IFS → data → ACK. No CTS or ACK → K + 1 → retry or abort. NAV = virtual carrier sense. RTS/CTS solves the hidden station problem.
  • Lecture slips to remember: RTS = Request To Send; K = 3 range is 0–7, not 0–8.
