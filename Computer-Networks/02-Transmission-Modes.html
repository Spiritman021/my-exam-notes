Chapter 2

Transmission Modes

Chapter 1 named the five components of data communication. This chapter answers a basic question about the direction of data flow between two devices: can data go one way only, both ways taking turns, or both ways at once?

2.0What is a Transmission Mode?

Transmission mode (also called data-flow direction or communication mode): the way data flows between two connected devices. There are three: simplex, half-duplex and full-duplex. It is a concern of the physical layer.

2.1Simplex Mode

Simplex: communication is unidirectional. One device can only send and the other can only receive; the roles never change. The entire channel capacity is used in one direction.
🎓 From the lecture — The husband-wife quarrel Data goes only one way, sender → receiver ("ek hi direction mein bhaagega"). When husband and wife quarrel, only the wife speaks and the husband sits quietly, always on the receiving end. "That's why life stays simple and durable." Trouble starts when it becomes half-duplex!
➕ Real examples Keyboard → CPU, CPU → monitor, TV and radio broadcasting, loudspeakers / public address systems, a traditional one-way pager, sensor → controller.

2.2Half-Duplex Mode

Half-duplex: communication is bidirectional, but one direction at a time. Each station can send and receive, but not simultaneously. While one sends, the other must only receive, and the entire channel capacity goes to the station transmitting at that moment.
🎓 From the lecture
  • Data can flow both ways: station S1 sends and S2 receives; later S2 sends and S1 receives. Each station can be sender or receiver.
  • But it is "one at a time": both can't send at the same moment.
  • The quarrel again: now both argue, with "teeron pe teer" (arrows after arrows), but in turns.
  • Walkie-talkie / radio jockey example from the movies: "Situation under control, over." Saying "over" hands the turn to the other side. Then you wait for their message.
➕ More examples Walkie-talkies, CB radio, police and army radios, hub-based Ethernet (shared medium; CSMA/CD handles collisions), and classic Wi-Fi channels (half-duplex on air).

2.3Full-Duplex Mode

Full-duplex (or simply duplex): communication is bidirectional and simultaneous. Both stations can send and receive at the same time. The channel capacity is shared between the two directions, either with separate paths or by dividing the capacity.
🎓 From the lecture — The road analogy
  • Half-duplex is like a narrow one-lane road: a vehicle can go this way or that way at one time. Driving both together isn't possible, so traffic and collisions build up.
  • Full-duplex has separate roads for coming and going ("aavan ke liye ek road, jaavan ke liye ek road"). Both stations send and receive at the same time, without any trouble, and there are very few traffic and collision problems.
➕ Examples and how it's done Telephone and mobile calls, video calls, switched Ethernet (full-duplex 100/1000 Mbps over separate wire pairs), fibre links, and the TCP connection itself (data flows both ways). It is implemented with two physically separate paths (e.g. separate wire pairs or fibres), or with one path whose capacity is divided (FDD or TDD in mobile networks).

🎯 IBPS exam alert "Walkie-talkie uses?" → half-duplex. "Telephone?" → full-duplex. "Keyboard to computer / TV broadcast?" → simplex. "Communication in both directions but not at the same time" → half-duplex.

2.4Serial, Parallel, Synchronous & Asynchronous Transmission ADDED

Some syllabi also list these under "transmission modes". They describe how bits are sent, not the direction.

TypeHowNotes / examples
Paralleln bits sent together over n wiresFast over short distances; costly; skew and crosstalk. Old printer (LPT) port, internal buses.
SerialOne bit at a time over one lineCheap, long distance; all modern links (USB, Ethernet, SATA)
↳ AsynchronousEach byte is framed with a start bit (0) and stop bit(s) (1); there may be gaps between bytesNo shared clock; cheap; 2–3 bits of overhead per byte. Keyboard, RS-232. "Asynchronous at the byte level, synchronous at the bit level."
↳ SynchronousA continuous bit stream in frames, with no start/stop bits and no gaps; sender and receiver share timingFast, efficient; needs clock synchronisation. Used in LAN/WAN links and leased lines.
↳ IsochronousGuaranteed fixed-rate delivery; no delay between framesReal-time audio and video (USB audio, VoIP)
Numerical: asynchronous overhead 8 data bits + 1 start + 1 stop = 10 bits per character.
Efficiency = 8 / 10 = 80%. At 9600 bps the effective throughput = 9600 × 0.8 = 7680 bps, i.e. 960 characters per second.

2.5Unicast, Multicast & Broadcast ADDED

TypeDeliveryExamples / addresses
UnicastOne sender → one specific receiverBrowsing a website, e-mail. A normal IP address.
MulticastOne sender → a selected groupIPTV, stock tickers, video conferencing. IPv4 Class D (224.0.0.0 – 239.255.255.255); managed by IGMP.
BroadcastOne sender → all nodes in the networkARP request, DHCP discover. 255.255.255.255; MAC FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF.
AnycastOne sender → the nearest member of a groupDNS root servers, CDNs (IPv6 supports it natively)
⚠️ Exam trap IPv6 has no broadcast. It uses multicast and anycast instead. Do not confuse the direction modes (simplex, half- and full-duplex) with the delivery types (unicast, multicast, broadcast).

⚡ Rapid Revision — Chapter 2

  • Simplex: one way only, fixed sender and receiver (only the wife speaks). Keyboard, TV, radio, monitor.
  • Half-duplex: both ways, one at a time; roles alternate ("over" on a walkie-talkie); single-lane road, so collisions are possible. Hub Ethernet.
  • Full-duplex: both ways simultaneously; separate paths ("aavan-jaavan" roads); best performance, no collisions. Telephone, mobile, switched Ethernet.
  • Channel capacity: simplex and half-duplex give the full capacity to one direction at a time; full-duplex shares it.
  • Parallel (n wires, short distance) vs serial (one wire). Serial is asynchronous (start/stop bits, 8/10 = 80% efficiency), synchronous (continuous frames, clocked) or isochronous (fixed rate, real-time).
  • Unicast 1→1 · multicast 1→group (Class D, IGMP) · broadcast 1→all (255.255.255.255, FF:FF…) · anycast 1→nearest. IPv6 has no broadcast.
