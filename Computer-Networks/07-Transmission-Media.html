Chapter 7

Transmission Media

Chapter 6 ended at the physical layer, which turns bits into signals. Those signals need something to travel through, which is the transmission medium. It sits below the physical layer, a kind of "layer 0".

7.0What is Transmission Media?

Transmission medium: the physical path or channel that carries signals from the sender to the receiver.
🎓 From the lecture It is the "ground reality, the actual truth": the physical path or channel that carries data from the sending end to the receiving end. Data goes down from the application layer to the physical layer, where the bits are encoded into compatible signals that the medium can carry. At the receiver it goes up layer by layer to the application layer. The medium is the connecting point in the middle.

7.1Guided Media

Guided (bounded / wired) media: the signal travels along a physical conductor (cable), which guides it along a fixed path.
🎓 From the lecture Guided media means wired, cables: the data follows the path of the cable. Three types: twisted pair, coaxial and optical fibre. "You are locked inside the cable": wherever the cable goes, the data goes.

7.2Twisted Pair Cable (10Base-T, 100Base-T)

🎓 From the lecture — Decoding "10Base-T"
  • 10 = the bandwidth / data rate, 10 Mbps.
  • Base = baseband (not broadband) transmission.
  • T: the lecture reads it as distance, a 100 m wire length.
  • Two flavours: 10Base-T and 100Base-T. The bandwidth rises to 100 Mbps, so more data in less time (a higher transfer rate).
⚠️ Exam trap (What "T" means) In 10Base-T, the "T" stands for Twisted pair (the medium), not distance. The maximum segment length is 100 m, which is why the lecture links T with 100 m. Only in the coaxial names (10Base2, 10Base5) does the last part give the length, in hundreds of metres.
➕ Key facts
  • Two insulated copper wires are twisted to reduce electromagnetic interference (EMI) and crosstalk.
  • UTP (Unshielded): cheap, flexible, the most common LAN cable. STP (Shielded): extra metal foil, better noise immunity, costlier.
  • Categories: CAT3 (10 Mbps, telephone) · CAT5 (100 Mbps) · CAT5e (1 Gbps) · CAT6 (1 Gbps, 10 Gbps over short runs) · CAT6a (10 Gbps, 100 m) · CAT7/8 (10–40 Gbps).
  • Connector: RJ-45 (8 pins, Ethernet), RJ-11 (telephone). Straight-through cable for unlike devices (PC ↔ switch); crossover for like devices (PC ↔ PC, switch ↔ switch).
  • Uses: telephone lines, DSL, Ethernet LANs. Limitations: attenuation over distance and limited bandwidth.

7.3Coaxial Cable (10Base2, 10Base5)

🎓 From the lecture Two variants: 10Base2 and 10Base5. Here the length is varied and the bandwidth is kept constant (10 Mbps), again baseband. 2 means about 200 m and 5 means 500 m of wire length. Moving from twisted pair to coaxial, the distance grows from 100 m to 200 m and then 500 m.
➕ Key facts
  • Structure: a central copper core, insulator, braided metal shield and outer jacket. It carries higher frequencies and resists noise better than twisted pair.
  • 10Base2 = "Thinnet" (RG-58): the exact maximum segment is 185 m (≈ 200). 10Base5 = "Thicknet" (RG-8): 500 m. Both were used in old bus Ethernet.
  • Connector: BNC (with T-connectors and terminators). RG-59/RG-6: cable TV and CCTV.
  • Uses: cable TV, the cable-Internet last mile, old LANs, CCTV.

7.4Optical Fibre Cable

🎓 From the lecture It reaches about 2 km, again baseband, with higher speed. It operates in full-duplex mode, and it has the greatest distance of all the cables mentioned.
➕ Key facts
  • It carries data as light pulses through a glass or plastic core surrounded by cladding of lower refractive index. Light stays inside by total internal reflection.
  • Single-mode: a very thin core (≈ 8–10 µm) and a laser source; one light path; very long distances (tens of km or more). Multimode: a thicker core (50/62.5 µm) and an LED source; many paths, causing modal dispersion; shorter distances (≈ 2 km). It comes as step-index and graded-index.
  • Advantages: the highest bandwidth (Gbps to Tbps), very low attenuation, immune to EMI, lightweight, secure (very hard to tap).
  • Disadvantages: costly, needs skilled installation and splicing, fragile, carries light in one direction only (so two fibres are used for full-duplex).
  • Connectors: SC, ST, LC. Uses: Internet backbones, undersea cables, FTTH, bank data-centre links.

7.5Unguided Media: Radio, Microwave and Infrared

Unguided (unbounded / wireless) media: signals travel through free space (air or vacuum) as electromagnetic waves, without a physical conductor. Transmission and reception happen through antennas.
🎓 From the lecture With guided media you're "locked in a cable". With unguided media there is free space, open air: data is transmitted through wireless signals and waves. Moving from radio → microwave → infrared, the frequency increases:
  • Radio waves: 3 kHz – 1 GHz
  • Microwaves: 1 GHz – 300 GHz
  • Infrared: 300 GHz – 400 THz
After infrared comes the visible spectrum, and beyond it UV with even higher frequency. Always remember: frequency is inversely proportional to wavelength. Where frequency rises, wavelength falls.
Numerical: c = f × λ (added) c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz: λ = 3×10⁸ / 2.4×10⁹ = 0.125 m (12.5 cm). FM radio at 100 MHz: λ = 3×10⁸ / 10⁸ = 3 m.
Conclusion: higher frequency means a shorter wavelength, so smaller antennas but more absorption by walls and obstacles.

7.6Radio Waves (Omnidirectional) vs Microwaves (Unidirectional)

🎓 From the lecture Radio waves are omnidirectional: they travel in every direction, so the sending and receiving antennas needn't be aligned ("antenna se antenna milao" isn't needed). Microwaves are unidirectional: on a very tall tower, the sending antenna is aligned with the receiving tower's antenna, and data goes in that one direction.

7.6APropagation, Satellite & Impairments ADDED

Propagation modeHowFrequency / use
Ground waveFollows the curvature of the EarthLow frequency (< 2 MHz): AM radio
Sky waveReflected by the ionosphere back to Earth2–30 MHz (HF): long-distance shortwave radio
Line of sightStraight line; antennas must "see" each other> 30 MHz: microwave, satellite, FM, TV
  • Communication satellite: a microwave relay in space. Uplink (Earth → satellite) and downlink use different frequencies. GEO (geostationary) is at 35,786 km and appears fixed in the sky; about 3 satellites cover the whole Earth. It has a delay of about 240–270 ms one way. LEO (e.g. Starlink) is at 500–2000 km with low delay. VSAT is used by banks for remote ATMs and branches.
  • Transmission impairments: attenuation (loss of signal strength with distance, fixed by repeaters/amplifiers; measured in decibels, dB = 10 log₁₀(P₂/P₁)), distortion (the signal's shape changes because components travel at different speeds), and noise (thermal, induced, crosstalk, impulse).
  • Data-rate limits: Nyquist (noiseless channel) gives bit rate = 2 × B × log₂L. Shannon (noisy channel) gives capacity C = B × log₂(1 + SNR). Example: B = 3000 Hz and SNR = 3162 (≈ 35 dB) give C ≈ 3000 × 11.63 ≈ 34.9 kbps.

⚡ Rapid Revision — Chapter 7

  • Medium = the physical path that carries the encoded signals ("ground reality"). Guided (wired): twisted pair, coaxial, fibre. Unguided (wireless): radio, microwave, infrared.
  • Twisted pair: 10Base-T / 100Base-T. 10 = Mbps, Base = baseband, T = twisted pair (max 100 m; the lecture reads T as 100 m). UTP/STP, CAT5e/6, RJ-45.
  • Coaxial: 10Base2 (≈ 200 m, really 185 m, thinnet) and 10Base5 (500 m, thicknet); 10 Mbps baseband; BNC; cable TV.
  • Optical fibre: light by total internal reflection; ≈ 2 km+ (the most distance), baseband, full-duplex, highest bandwidth, EMI-immune, secure. Single-mode (laser, long) vs multimode (LED, short).
  • Spectrum: radio 3 kHz–1 GHz → microwave 1–300 GHz → infrared 300 GHz–400 THz → visible → UV. Frequency ∝ 1/wavelength (c = fλ).
  • Radio = omnidirectional (no alignment, passes walls). Microwave = unidirectional (aligned tower antennas, line of sight, satellite, mobile). Infrared = TV remote, blocked by walls.
  • Propagation: ground, sky (ionosphere), line of sight. GEO satellite at 35,786 km. Impairments: attenuation, distortion, noise. Shannon C = B log₂(1+SNR).
