Chapter 9

Network Devices

Chapter 7 covered the cables. This chapter covers the boxes that connect them: repeaters and hubs (layer 1), bridges and switches (layer 2), and routers (layer 3). Each step up the OSI stack adds more intelligence: from blindly forwarding everything, to filtering by MAC address, to routing by IP address. That decides how many devices can collide and how far a broadcast can travel.

9.1Repeater

Repeater: a physical-layer (L1) device with two ports that receives a signal, regenerates it, and sends it out the other side, extending the length of a network segment.
🎓 From the lecture
  • It joins two bus segments: one port on each side, so it is a two-port device.
  • It is pure hardware: no filtering, no intelligent software. It simply does forwarding.
  • Example: a station on the left sends data meant for another station on the same left segment. The repeater doesn't know that, so it forwards it to the right segment too, unnecessarily.

9.2Repeater: Signal Regeneration and Attenuation

🎓 From the lecture A signal's strength falls as it travels: strong at the start, weaker and weaker, and by about 100 m (the cable limit from Chapter 7) it is nearly dead. This loss is attenuation. The repeater regenerates the signal back to its original strength, and only that much. "Repeaters are NOT amplifiers." They don't boost beyond the original; they restore it.
➕ Regenerator vs amplifier An amplifier boosts the whole analog signal, including the noise. A repeater reads the digital bit pattern and creates a fresh, clean copy, so the noise is removed. Classic Ethernet's 5-4-3 rule: at most 5 segments, 4 repeaters, and only 3 segments populated with hosts in one collision domain.

9.3Repeater: Collision Domain

🎓 From the lecture Because the repeater doesn't filter, a signal from one side reaches the other side too. If any host on either side transmits at the same time, there is a collision. Collisions can happen on both ends: with n devices, all n can be involved in a collision.
⚠️ Exam trap (How to count collision domains) The lecture says "n devices, so the collision domain is n", meaning n devices are inside it. In MCQs the question is "how many collision domains?" A repeater (or hub) and everything connected to it form ONE collision domain. Layer-1 devices do not split collision domains.

9.4Hub (Multiport Repeater)

Hub: a multiport repeater (L1). A signal arriving on any port is repeated out of all other ports, with no filtering.
🎓 From the lecture A hub is also pure hardware, but a repeater has only 2 ports: "why only two? I'll connect 1, 2, 3, 4…". A multiport repeater = hub, an advanced repeater. It forwards / broadcasts: data from the sender goes to 2, 3 and 4, everyone. There is no filtering (it doesn't send only to the receiver). If several stations transmit together they collide; with all 4 transmitting, no data survives. The lecture counts the collision domain as n devices.
➕ Types and limits Passive hub: just connects, no regeneration. Active hub: regenerates (a true multiport repeater). Intelligent hub: adds monitoring. Bandwidth is shared among all ports (a 100 Mbps hub with 10 PCs gives about 10 Mbps each); half-duplex only; insecure (everyone sees all traffic). Hubs are now obsolete, replaced by switches.

9.5Bridge

Bridge: a data-link-layer (L2) device (it also works at L1) that connects two LAN segments and filters traffic using MAC addresses stored in a table. It is store-and-forward: it receives the whole frame, checks it, then forwards or discards it.
🎓 From the lecture — The river bridge Road 1 and Road 2 are on opposite banks of a river; a bridge connects them. Here the "roads" are two LANs.
  • It has hardware + software. It works on store-and-forward: the message comes in, is stored, the bridge consults a static table, and then filters or forwards.
  • It works at the physical and data link layers and uses MAC addresses, which are listed in the table.
  • Table: M1, M2, M3 → port 1 (LAN 1); M4, M5 → port 2 (LAN 2).
  • M1 sends to M2. The bridge sees that the source and destination are both on port 1, so there is no need to forward to port 2. It filters the frame. A hub or repeater would have forwarded it.
  • So the collision domain is reduced compared with a hub or repeater.
⚠️ Exam trap (Static vs learning bridge) The lecture shows a static table. Real bridges are transparent / learning bridges: they build the table automatically by reading the source MAC of each incoming frame. If the destination is unknown, they flood the frame to all other ports. Loops between bridges are prevented by the Spanning Tree Protocol (STP, IEEE 802.1D). Each bridge port is a separate collision domain, so a 2-port bridge gives 2 collision domains and 1 broadcast domain.

9.6Collision Domain

Collision domain: a network segment in which frames from two devices can collide if they transmit at the same time. All devices in it share one medium; only one can transmit successfully at a time.
🎓 From the lecture — The teacher and four questions Stations 1, 2, 3, 4 on a bus all transmit together, so a collision happens. In the worst case all four lose their data, so "the collision domain is 4". Real life: the teacher sits in the centre to hear questions, and you and three friends all speak at once. The voice signals mix, and the teacher can't answer anyone. Four people's queries go unresolved: that's the collision domain.

9.7Broadcast Domain

Broadcast domain: the set of all devices that receive a broadcast frame sent by any one of them. A broadcast can reach everyone in it.
🎓 From the lecture — The election rally Station 1 broadcasts a message over the LAN, and stations 2, 3, 4 (every machine) receive it. Only the intended one (say station 2) accepts it; the others discard it. But it reached everyone, so that is the broadcast domain.
Like a leader at an election rally with a mic: the speech reaches the entire crowd, not one person (unicast).
Hubs, repeaters and switches don't reduce the broadcast domain. A router reduces it: it stops one network's broadcast from crossing into another network, which also reduces congestion.

9.8Switch (Multiport Bridge)

Switch: a multiport bridge (L2). It learns MAC addresses and sends each frame only to the port of the destination (unicast), using full-duplex dedicated links to each device.
🎓 From the lecture A bridge has 2 ports connecting 2 LANs: "why only two?" Just as a multiport repeater became a hub, a multiport bridge is a switch, with more than 2 ports and many kinds of device attached. Very important: here the "collision domain is ZERO". Why? Full-duplex links: separate paths for coming and going ("aavan-jaavan ke alag raaste"), so there is nowhere to collide. Traffic is also well managed and low.
⚠️ Exam trap (Switch collision domains) The lecture's "zero" means no collisions occur on full-duplex switch links. In counting questions, each switch port is its own collision domain: a 24-port switch with 24 PCs has 24 collision domains and 1 broadcast domain. Switches do not break broadcast domains (unless VLANs are configured; each VLAN is its own broadcast domain).
➕ Switching methods and types
  • Store-and-forward: receive the whole frame, check its CRC, then forward. Reliable but slower.
  • Cut-through: forward as soon as the destination MAC (the first 6 bytes) is read. Fastest, but errors pass through.
  • Fragment-free: read the first 64 bytes (the collision window), then forward. A middle ground.
  • Layer-3 switch: a switch with routing capability (inter-VLAN routing). Managed vs unmanaged switches. The table is the MAC / CAM table.

9.9Router

Router: a network-layer (L3) device that connects different networks and forwards packets between them using IP addresses and a routing table, choosing the best path.
🎓 From the lecture A router has three layers: physical, data link and network. (Some switches also work at the network layer.) It deals with IP addresses and creates routing tables; routing algorithms come later in Chapter 27. It does forwarding, filtering, and flooding. Its main job: connect two networks and forward packets from one network to the other.
➕ Key facts
  • Every router interface is a separate broadcast domain and collision domain. Routers do not forward broadcasts.
  • It works with different technologies (Ethernet ↔ WAN links), performs NAT, and runs routing protocols (RIP, OSPF, BGP).
  • A home "Wi-Fi router" is really a router + switch + access point + modem in one box.

9.10Flooding vs Routing

🎓 From the lecture Flooding: a packet arrives on an incoming link, and the router places it on every possible outgoing link. It is a brute-force method ("somehow it'll reach the destination"), but it increases traffic congestion. Routing: a sophisticated method. The router consults its routing table and sends the packet only along the best, optimal path from source to destination.
➕ Where flooding is still useful It always finds the shortest path (some copy takes it) and is very robust, so it suits military networks and the distribution of routing information (OSPF's link-state flooding). It is controlled with a hop count / TTL, sequence numbers (don't flood the same packet twice), or selective flooding (only on links in roughly the right direction).

⚡ Rapid Revision — Chapter 9

  • Repeater (L1, 2 ports, pure hardware): no filtering, just forwards (even to the side that doesn't need it). Fixes attenuation by regenerating to the original strength; it is NOT an amplifier.
  • Hub = multiport repeater (L1): broadcasts every frame to all ports; collisions; textbook: 1 collision domain and 1 broadcast domain (the lecture says "n devices involved").
  • Bridge (L1 + L2, hardware + software): connects 2 LANs (the river bridge), store-and-forward, uses a MAC table (M1–M3 on port 1, M4–M5 on port 2). M1 → M2 is filtered. Reduces collision domains. Real bridges learn; STP prevents loops.
  • Collision domain: devices whose frames can collide (four students speak together, the teacher answers no one). Broadcast domain: everyone a broadcast reaches (the election rally). Only routers cut broadcast domains.
  • Switch = multiport bridge: full-duplex dedicated links, so no collisions (the lecture's "zero"; in counting, one collision domain per port, 1 broadcast domain). Store-and-forward, cut-through, fragment-free.
  • Router (L1–3): IP addresses, routing table, forwards, filters and floods; connects networks; each interface = its own broadcast domain.
  • Flooding = copy on every link (brute force, congestion; limit with TTL). Routing = the best path via the routing table.
  • Gateway (all layers, protocol conversion), modem, NIC, AP, brouter, firewall.
